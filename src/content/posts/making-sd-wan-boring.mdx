I've designed and implemented several large Cisco Meraki SD-WAN deployments
now, across estates big enough that no single person could have touched every
site. The honest summary of all of them is that the interesting part was not
Meraki.

The overlay is genuinely good, and I'd choose it again. But if I had to
explain why those migrations went smoothly, almost none of the reasons would
be features. They'd be things like knowing which of our "redundant" circuits
actually shared a conduit, or having the new edge sitting on the LAN
answering pings for two weeks before it carried a packet of production
traffic. The goal was to make a WAN migration across dozens of sites feel
repetitive, and mostly we got there.

What follows is the pattern that kept showing up.

## Fix the underlay first

Before we deployed anything, we audited the ISP connectivity at every site.
Not a spreadsheet of "site, carrier, speed" — an actual audit. Carrier,
bandwidth, circuit type, who owns the contract, renewal date, monthly cost,
public IP allocation, handoff type, and then the question that matters most:
are the two circuits at this site genuinely independent?

That last one has a specific failure mode. A site has two carriers, two
invoices, two demarcs, and a diagram with two lines on it — and both circuits
ride the same last mile, or land in the same carrier POP, or one carrier is
quietly reselling the other's access. Having two ISP invoices does not
necessarily mean you have two independent WAN paths. You find this out either
by asking hard questions during an audit or by watching both circuits drop
at the same moment on a Tuesday afternoon.

For several deployments we worked through ISP brokers — Global Gig among them
— to centralize carrier management. They handled sourcing, diversity
analysis, rate negotiation, contract consolidation, permits, install
scheduling, IP block approvals, the endless carrier follow-up, project
management, testing and final handoff. That is a real category of work, and
it is not work my engineers should be doing.

Sometimes the audit ended with adding a second ISP. Sometimes it ended with
replacing the primary too, because the incumbent circuit was the weakest
thing at the site. Either way the point is the same: SD-WAN cannot
manufacture resilience out of two bad circuits. It can steer around a brownout
beautifully, but only if there is somewhere to steer to.

## The bill was the easy win

We were overpaying for a lot of circuits. Looking at you, AT&T.

The pattern was consistent enough to be funny. In many cases we could buy a
brand-new circuit from the *same carrier*, at a higher speed tier, for a
lower monthly rate than the legacy circuit we were replacing. That sounds
absurd until you remember how enterprise telecom pricing works: a contract
signed years ago and quietly auto-renewed since is priced against a market
that no longer exists. Nobody at the carrier is going to call and offer you
the better deal.

So the migrations often landed on more bandwidth, better diversity, lower
monthly cost and fewer vendors to chase, all at once. The bandwidth increase
alone changed what was possible at some sites.

For some deployments the broker also provided monitored and managed ISP
services, which meant they owned the carrier-facing operational work after
deployment. Our NMS detected the circuit failure; they opened the ticket, sat
on hold, worked through the ISP's script, chased updates, coordinated with
the last-mile provider and escalated the POP or local-loop problems. If the
NMS sees a circuit go down — hello, Stratora — I don't necessarily need one
of my engineers spending an hour proving to a carrier that their own circuit
is down.

That division of labor makes more sense the moment you look at where these
failures actually live. It's rarely inside the building. It's the last mile,
the carrier POP, a fiber cut two towns over, an access-layer provider fault,
carrier CPE, or something upstream that nobody will describe to you
precisely. Our job is to survive it; the carrier's job is to fix it.

## Not every branch needs two DIA circuits

Moving away from MPLS saved real money in most cases and usually came with a
substantial bandwidth increase. That isn't a knock on MPLS — it did something
genuinely useful for a long time, offering predictable private connectivity
with behavior you could plan around. But encrypted overlays and local
Internet breakout changed the economics underneath it, and paying private-
circuit prices to backhaul a branch's cloud traffic through a data center
stopped making sense.

What replaced it was usually simpler than what people expect. A very
practical branch design looked like this: WAN 1 a DIA circuit, WAN 2 FTTX or
business broadband, WAN 3 cellular. Three independent transports, an
encrypted corporate overlay across them, and local breakout for the traffic
that has no business crossing the country first.

In practice that often delivered better resiliency than the legacy WAN while
costing less, because the failure domains were genuinely different rather
than nominally different. DIA still earns its place where SLAs, symmetry or
specific critical services justify it, and we kept it where that was true.
The point was never that broadband beats DIA. The point is to pick transports
that suit the site instead of reflexively buying two expensive DIA circuits
everywhere because that's what the template said.

## Cellular as insurance, not as a circuit

In the newer deployments we used Meraki MG cellular gateways, and the
difference from some of our older cellular designs was mostly operational.
We've built tertiary cellular with CradlePoint, carrier-supplied modems and
assorted third-party LTE and 5G devices, and all of it worked. What the MG
changed was that cellular stopped being its own little island with its own
portal, its own firmware story and its own troubleshooting ritual.

It showed up in the same dashboard as everything else: same inventory, same
configuration model, same visibility, same remote deployment story, same
place to look when something is wrong. Across dozens of sites, that
consistency is worth more than any individual feature. Less vendor sprawl
means fewer things a tired engineer has to remember at 2 a.m.

<TriWan />

The other half of making tertiary cellular viable is the data plan, and this
is the part I'd tell anyone to ask about explicitly: ask your broker or
carrier about pooled data across the estate. Most of those cellular circuits
will do nothing all year. Paying for a large standalone plan at every branch
to cover an outage that may never come is how tertiary connectivity gets cut
from the budget. A pooled allocation shared across many sites prices it like
what it actually is — an insurance layer for full terrestrial outages, fiber
cuts, maintenance windows, local carrier failures and facility incidents.

Designed that way, cellular at a lot of critical sites is surprisingly
affordable. Designed as a primary circuit at every site, it isn't.

## Build the edge so the redundancy survives

At most sites we used Meraki appliances in HA pairs, and we physically
separated the WAN handoffs. WAN 1 to DMZ switch 1, WAN 2 to DMZ switch 2,
WAN 3 or cellular to a third where one was present — with both appliances
able to reach every available uplink.

<BranchHa />

This is the part that gets skipped when a project is behind schedule, and
it's the part I care about most. It is entirely possible to buy two diverse
circuits, pay for them every month, then land them both on the same switch
and spend the next three years believing you have redundancy. The hardware
specifics varied across deployments, but the pattern didn't: no single
switch, port group or appliance should be able to take down more paths than
it has any right to.

With the terrestrial circuits up, tunnels ran active/active across them.
Where tertiary cellular was present, the behavior was effectively
active/active/passive — cellular staying out of the way until it was the only
thing left, exactly as intended.

## Prestage everything

A lot of branch and ROBO sites had no local IT, so we prestaged everything
in-house. We claimed and configured the devices, labeled the hardware and the
ports, prebuilt the configurations, generated a connection diagram specific
to that site, included color-coded CAT6 patch cables, packed it all logically
and shipped it out.

The target was that the box arriving at the site contained a mostly assembled
answer rather than a network engineering project. Whoever opened it —
sometimes an office manager, sometimes a general contractor's electrician —
should be plugging colored cable into matching colored labels, not
interpreting anything.

Where a site did have competent local IT, we used a different trick: connect
the new appliances behind the existing network, temporarily double-NATted,
and let them reach the cloud and pull their configuration days before the
cutover. That gave us cloud registration, configuration state, uplink status,
firmware and basic tunnel readiness all confirmed while production routing
sat untouched. Most of the things that go wrong on cutover night are things
that could have been discovered a week earlier, for free.

## One address, at every site

This is almost too simple to write down, and it made everything easier.

The existing WAN routers already had LAN-side addresses at each site, so we
standardized where the new edge would live in that space:

```
x.x.x.1     core gateway VIP
x.x.x.2     core switch 1
x.x.x.3     core switch 2
x.x.x.4-5   legacy WAN router(s)
x.x.x.254   Meraki HA VRRP VIP
```

We used `.254` where it was free, because it usually was. The specific number
doesn't matter at all. The consistency does: an engineer working any site in
the estate knows where the SD-WAN edge lives without opening documentation,
and every runbook, diagram and rollback note says the same thing.

More importantly, this let the legacy WAN and the new SD-WAN coexist on the
same LAN. Both were cabled, both were live, and we could validate Internet
access, tunnel establishment, failover behavior, routing and application
reachability from the new path while production kept running on the old one.

## The cutover is a routing change

At a typical branch, the migration itself should sound anticlimactic, because
it was. The core already had a default route pointing at the legacy WAN next
hop. At cutover, that became the Meraki VRRP VIP.

<Coexistence />

That was the meaningful change. No endpoint readdressing, no gateway
migration, no rebuilding core VLANs, no pulling the old routers out of the
rack before anything was proven. The legacy WAN stayed connected and stayed
capable. If validation went badly enough, rollback was changing the default
route back.

I like migrations where rollback can be explained in one sentence. It changes
the conversation with the business, it changes how willing people are to
schedule the window, and it changes how calm the room is at 9 p.m.

## Standardize, but don't pretend every site is the same

Branches and data centers didn't get the same routing design, and they
shouldn't have. A simple branch has a handful of VLANs that change maybe
twice a decade, so static routes on the Meraki side pointed at the core LAN
VIP were exactly enough — fewer moving parts, nothing to converge, nothing to
misconfigure. Larger facilities and data centers, where subnets and VLANs
change often enough to make static routing a maintenance liability, got OSPF
between the core and the WAN edge.

Overlay topology followed the same logic. For large environments with many
ROBOs, geographic hubs made sense: concentrate the tunnels, concentrate the
services behind them, keep the fabric comprehensible. For some international
environments with fewer than roughly fifty locations, fuller mesh designs
were the better fit — particularly where staff and management routinely
traveled between facilities and worked out of whichever office they were in
that week. When people genuinely move between sites, direct site-to-site
reachability stops being an architectural nicety.

Neither answer is universally right. The architecture should follow how the
organization actually communicates, which is a question you answer by looking
at traffic and asking people, not by picking the topology you like.

A ten-person sales office and a data center do not need identical routing
designs just because they both happen to run Meraki.

## Designing the in-between

Large migrations don't happen at once, so for a long stretch all of it
coexists: SD-WAN, MPLS, legacy IPsec tunnels, old static routes and whatever
site-specific dependency somebody built in 2016. That transitional state is
not an accident to be endured. It's a thing you design.

For the hybrid MPLS and IPsec period, selected hub and data-center locations
advertised or redistributed reachability between the legacy WAN and the
SD-WAN fabric. The East data center acted as the next hop and redistribution
point for the remaining East Coast MPLS sites; the West data center did the
same for western locations.

<HybridTransition />

That let migrated sites keep reaching not-yet-migrated ones for as long as
the rollout took, which in practice meant nobody outside the project team
needed to know or care what order the sites were done in. As each site cut
over, the legacy domain got a little smaller, until eventually the
redistribution had nothing left to redistribute and could come out.

Designing the temporary state properly is just as important as designing the
final one. You will live in the temporary state far longer.

## When vMX wasn't an option

One of the larger global deployments introduced a different kind of problem.
We needed the SD-WAN fabric to reach several major data centers, and the
obvious answer — put a vMX in front of them so they join the fabric like any
other site — wasn't available to us. At the time of that project, vMX
deployment was limited to the supported public cloud platforms: AWS, Azure,
Google Cloud and Alibaba Cloud. The data-center environment we needed to
connect wasn't one of them. That constraint was specific to that project and
that point in time, and it's the kind of thing worth re-checking rather than
assuming.

We could have stood up a public-cloud presence purely to have somewhere to
host a headend, but building out a cloud footprint to solve a routing problem
for data centers that already existed is a strange amount of tail wagging a
fairly large dog. So we bridged the two environments differently, using Cisco
Secure Access as the connection point between the data centers and the Meraki
estate.

The data-center side of it is conventional, which is most of the appeal. Each
data center brought up redundant IPsec tunnels toward Cisco Secure Access and
ran eBGP across them, advertising its internal prefixes. Secure Access
propagated those routes into the Meraki-connected environment, and routes
from the SD-WAN side could be exchanged back toward the data centers where
that made sense. Standard IPsec, standard eBGP, nothing exotic terminating on
the data-center edge.

<SecureAccessHeadend />

What that bought us, beyond simply having a path, was not maintaining a large
static route matrix between the data centers and the SD-WAN estate. Static
routing at that scale is fine right up until the first subnet changes, at
which point it becomes a permanent low-grade tax on everyone who touches the
environment. Dynamic routing handled the exchange, and new data-center
prefixes showed up in the fabric without anyone editing anything.

The organization had locations across the United States, Canada, Mexico,
Europe and APAC, so the design was deliberately regional. Cisco Secure Access
offers connection points in many locations, and we paired them geographically
rather than pointing everything at one place. Northeast sites preferred New
York with Ashburn as the redundant region. Canadian sites used Toronto with
Vancouver. West Coast locations used western regions such as Palo Alto and
Los Angeles. The other regions followed the same logic, chosen on locality,
latency and having the second choice fail in a different way than the first.

The principle was that local sites should use nearby Secure Access regions
rather than hairpinning traffic across a continent to reach a headend, while
Secure Access itself acted as the dynamic route-exchange bridge into the
major data centers. These are Cisco-hosted locations we connected into, not
infrastructure we operated — which is the point. We got redundant regional
termination and geographic resiliency without racking anything or taking on
another environment to run.

The part I didn't fully appreciate until we were running on it is that Secure
Access isn't only a set of regional termination points. Its locations are
interconnected, so once traffic is inside the fabric, Cisco's backbone becomes
available as another transport domain between regions. That changes the shape
of long-haul traffic for a company with sites in the US, Canada, Mexico,
Europe and APAC. An APAC office reaching something in the East Coast data
center no longer depends entirely on whatever end-to-end Internet path it can
get across the Pacific, or on whichever route our own overlay happens to
produce that day. It can enter Secure Access locally, cross the backbone, and
come out near the destination before making the IPsec and eBGP handoff into
the data center.

I want to be careful about how strongly I put that. This isn't a claim that
every packet rides Cisco's backbone, or that Secure Access replaces Internet
transit, or that you get a latency win in every direction — the honest
version is that it gave us another way to carry long-distance traffic between
regions, and for the intercontinental pairs that mattered it behaved better
than relying solely on a public path from one side of the world to the other.
On a global estate, having that option at all is worth something.

It also kept a clean line between the two routing domains. The branch SD-WAN
fabric stayed a branch SD-WAN fabric; the data centers kept their own routing
domain and their own edge; and the two exchanged exactly what they needed to
exchange over a mechanism that scaled as more regions came online. That
separation is worth as much as the connectivity, because it means neither
side's future changes are automatically the other side's problem.

The same design leaves an obvious next step sitting there on the remote-access
side, which we haven't taken yet. If client VPN moves into Secure Access,
roaming users land on the region closest to them instead of tunnelling back
to a fixed concentrator. That matters when the company is spread across five
regions: a user in Singapore who needs something hosted in Singapore or Tokyo
currently has a decent chance of crossing the Pacific twice to get it —
out to a concentrator, then back again — which is a lot of geography for a
file share.

<FutureRemoteAccess />

Whether it delivers depends on the specifics, like anything else; the point
is that the architecture we built for the data centers happens to be the same
architecture remote access would want, and that isn't always how these things
line up.

## Test the rollback, not just the rollout

Pilot with small sites first. Then pilot with the awkward ones — the site
with the ancient application nobody owns, the site with the strange voice
setup, the site where the local Internet is genuinely bad. Then scale.

At each stage we validated the same list: ISP failover, appliance failover,
tunnel failover, routing, Internet access, internal application reachability,
voice, DNS, whatever site-specific services existed, cellular failover where
present, and rollback. Pulling cables to watch what actually happens is worth
more than any amount of confidence about what should happen.

That last item deserves its own sentence. A rollback plan that has never been
exercised is mostly a theory. We tested reverting the default route as
deliberately as we tested the cutover, because the entire premise of the
low-drama migration is that the escape hatch works.

By the time we were dozens of sites in, the process felt repetitive. Same
prestage, same shipment, same validation, same one-line change. That's not
boredom; that's the product. Repetitive means predictable, and predictable is
what lets you schedule forty cutovers without forty separate arguments.

## What actually made it work

None of the individual pieces here are exotic. Cleaning up the carrier
underlay and insisting on real path diversity. Taking the cost savings that
were sitting there. Prestaging hardware so site visits were assembly rather
than engineering. Standardized addressing. Coexistence on the LAN. Routing
designs that suited the site instead of the template. Hub topology that
matched how the company actually works. Transitional routing that was
deliberately designed. A data-center bridge built out of standard IPsec and
eBGP when the tidy option wasn't available to us. Cellular treated as
estate-wide insurance. Extensive testing, including of the rollback.

Meraki made the overlay straightforward, and that matters — it's why the
fabric was the least eventful part of the project. But the reason these
deployments went well is that the architecture around it kept the number of
things changing at any one moment very small.

By the time we changed the default route, most of the project was already
over.
